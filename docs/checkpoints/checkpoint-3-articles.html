How to Use a French Press: A Step-by-Step Guide to a Clean, Full-Bodied Cup

Passed the gate Repaired once Review 5/4/4/4

To use a French press, combine coarsely ground coffee with water just off the boil at a ratio of roughly 1:15 to 1:16, steep for about 4 minutes, skim the crust, plunge slowly and pour everything out at once. Get those variables right and you get a heavy, rounded cup without a layer of sludge at the bottom. Below we walk through each variable, the full brewing sequence, how to choose beans, and how to keep the press clean so it keeps tasting good.

Why the French press tastes the way it does

A French press is an immersion brewer. Instead of water flowing through a bed of coffee, as in pour-over, all of the grounds sit in all of the water for the whole brew. Extraction happens evenly and fairly gently, and steep time, rather than flow rate, becomes your main control.

When the steep is finished, a metal mesh filter pushes the grounds to the bottom of the beaker. Unlike paper, mesh lets coffee oils and some very fine particles pass into the cup. That is where the French press gets its heavier body and fuller texture. The trade-off is that those same fines can make the cup muddy if the grind or technique is off, which is what most of this guide is about.

What you need: ratio, dose and batch size

A reliable starting point is a ratio of 1:15 to 1:16 by weight, coffee to water. That works out to roughly 63–67 g of coffee per litre of water. Use the lower end of the range for a stronger cup and the higher end for something lighter.

Scaled to smaller batches, the same ratio looks like this:

  • 250 ml of water: 16–17 g of coffee
  • 500 ml of water: 31–33 g of coffee
  • 750 ml of water: 47–50 g of coffee
  • 1,000 ml of water: 63–67 g of coffee

Weigh both coffee and water rather than relying on scoops. Scoop volume varies with bean size, density and roast level, and a few grams either way changes strength noticeably. The simplest method is to place the beaker on a scale, add the coffee, tare, and pour water straight to the target weight.

Our French Press has a capacity of 1 litre and makes about 4 mugs. You do not have to fill a press to capacity every time; keep the ratio constant and the cup will stay consistent whatever the batch size.

Grind coarse and grind fresh

Grind size does two jobs in a French press. A coarse grind slows extraction, which matters when the coffee and water are in contact for several minutes. It also produces fewer fines, the dust-like particles that slip through mesh and cloud the cup.

Evenness matters as much as coarseness. Burr grinders cut beans to a fairly consistent size, while blade grinders shatter them into a mix of dust and large chunks. That mix over-extracts and under-extracts at the same time, giving a cup that is both bitter and thin, with sediment on top.

Grind just before you brew. Ground coffee loses aromatics quickly, and the difference shows up clearly in a brew method that carries as much body and flavour as immersion does.

Step by step: brewing your French press

The steps below are written for an immersion brewer with a mesh filter, such as our French Press.

  • Preheat. Rinse the beaker with hot water and discard it. A warm beaker keeps the brew temperature from dropping as soon as you pour.
  • Add the grounds. Weigh your freshly ground, coarse coffee into the beaker and tare the scale.
  • Pour. Use water just off the boil, roughly 93–96 °C. Pour fairly quickly so all the grounds are saturated at once, and stop at your target weight.
  • Stir. Give the slurry a gentle stir so there are no dry clumps. Some brewers prefer to pour a little water first, let it bloom briefly, then add the rest; either approach works if everything ends up wet.
  • Steep. Set the lid on top with the plunger raised and steep for about 4 minutes.
  • Break the crust and skim. By the end of the steep a crust of grounds floats on the surface. Stir it gently so most of the grounds sink, then spoon off the foam and any bits still floating. This removes much of the material that would otherwise end up as sediment in your mug.
  • Wait (optional). Leaving the coffee a few more minutes lets more fines settle to the bottom before you plunge. The cup gets noticeably cleaner, and because the grounds have sunk, extraction slows considerably.
  • Plunge slowly. Press the plunger down gently and steadily. The aim is to lower the mesh to sit just above the settled grounds, not to compress them. Plunging hard stirs up the bed and forces fines through the filter.
  • Pour all of it. Decant everything into mugs or a separate carafe straight away. Coffee left sitting on the grounds keeps extracting and turns bitter and harsh. Stop before the last dregs, which hold the most sediment.

Choosing beans for the French press

The heavy body of immersion brewing pairs well with coffees that have sweetness and depth. Medium roasts with chocolate and caramel notes tend to shine here, because the oils and texture the mesh lets through support those flavours. Washed coffees give a clean, sweet base that keeps a full-bodied cup from tasting muddy.

Adjusting by taste

Once you have a baseline, change one variable at a time:

  • Sour, sharp or thin: the coffee is under-extracted. Grind a little finer or steep a little longer.
  • Bitter, dry or harsh: the coffee is over-extracted. Grind a little coarser or shorten the steep.
  • Muddy or gritty: grind coarser, skim more thoroughly, wait longer before plunging, and plunge more gently.
  • Weak but balanced: use more coffee for the same amount of water.

Cleaning and care

A French press only tastes as clean as its filter. Coffee oils collect in the mesh and around the plunger, and over time they turn rancid and give fresh brews a stale, bitter edge.

  • Dispose of grounds properly. Do not wash them down the sink, where they can build up in the drain. Scoop them into compost or the bin, then rinse what remains.
  • Rinse after every brew. Run hot water through the mesh and over the plunger while the oils are still fresh and easy to remove.
  • Disassemble regularly. Take the plunger and filter apart and wash each layer with warm, soapy water, then rinse well and let everything dry before reassembling.

All parts of our French Press come apart for cleaning. Its beaker is borosilicate glass, and it is replaceable.

If a glass beaker cracks or breaks, stop using it, collect the pieces carefully, and fit a replacement before brewing again. To reduce the risk, avoid pouring hot water into a beaker that is very cold, and do not knock the glass against the tap or sink edge while rinsing.

Beyond hot brew: cold brew by immersion

The same immersion principle works with cold water. Coarse grounds steep in cold water in the fridge for many hours, producing a smooth concentrate that you dilute to taste. You can do this in a French press, but it ties up your hot brewer for most of a day, and a larger dedicated vessel makes more concentrate per batch.

How to Make Cold Brew Coffee at Home: Beans, Grind, Ratio and Steep Time

Passed the gate First draft Review 5/4/5/5

To make cold brew coffee, steep coarsely ground coffee in cold water for many hours, then separate the grounds and dilute the concentrate to taste. Most recipes call for somewhere between 12 and 24 hours of steeping. The variables that decide whether you get a smooth, chocolatey concentrate or a flat, bitter one are the beans, the grind, the ratio of coffee to water and the steep time. This guide covers each of them, then walks through the process step by step.

What cold brew is and why it tastes different

Cold brew is an immersion method. Instead of water passing through a bed of coffee, as in pour-over, the grounds sit fully submerged in cold or room-temperature water for hours rather than minutes.

Temperature changes what ends up in the cup. Hot water extracts acids and aromatic compounds quickly, which is why a hot filter can taste bright and lively. Cold water extracts far more slowly and pulls out less of that bright acidity, so cold brew tends to taste rounder, smoother and less sharp.

This is also the difference between cold brew and iced coffee. Iced coffee is brewed hot and then chilled or poured over ice, so it keeps the acidity of a hot brew. Cold brew never sees hot water at all, which gives it its softer, heavier character.

Choosing beans for cold brew

Cold brew mutes acidity and emphasises body and sweetness, so the coffee you pick shapes the result more than you might expect.

  • Medium roasts with chocolate and caramel character are the classic, forgiving choice. They give a sweet, rounded concentrate that works well black or with milk.
  • Fruity light roasts give a brighter, more unusual cup. Expect berry and floral notes rather than a straight chocolate profile.

Grind coarse, like for French press

Grind size matters more in cold brew than in almost any other method, because the coffee is in contact with water for so long.

  • Too fine: the grounds over-extract, the concentrate tastes bitter and harsh, and fine particles make it muddy and hard to filter.
  • Too coarse: too little is extracted and the concentrate comes out thin and weak, even after a long steep.

Aim for a coarse grind, similar to what you would use for a French press: chunky particles roughly the texture of coarse sea salt. Consistency matters as much as the setting, since a burr grinder produces more even particles than a blade grinder.

How much coffee and how long to steep

Cold brew is usually made as a concentrate, which you then dilute when serving. As a general guide, a concentrate uses roughly 1:5 to 1:8 coffee to water by weight. A ready-to-drink cold brew that needs no dilution sits closer to 1:12 to 1:15.

Weigh both the coffee and the water rather than measuring by volume. Scoops of coarse coffee vary a lot in weight, and weighing is the only way to repeat a batch you liked or to change one thing at a time when you adjust.

Coffee Grind Size Chart: The Right Grind for Pour-Over, French Press and Cold Brew

Passed the gate First draft Review 4/4/5/4

A coffee grind size chart matches each brewing method to a particle size. Use extra fine for Turkish coffee, fine for espresso, medium-fine for cone pour-over, medium for flat-bottom drippers, coarse for French press and extra coarse for cold brew. The principle behind it is simple: the longer water stays in contact with the coffee, the coarser the grind should be. Below you will find the chart, how to recognise each size by eye, and how to fine-tune it on a burr grinder by tasting the cup.

The coffee grind size chart at a glance

Grind size Looks and feels like Brewing method Contact time
Extra fine Flour or icing sugar Turkish coffee Very short, brought to the boil
Fine Finer than table salt, clumps when pinched Espresso Seconds under pressure
Medium-fine Table salt Cone pour-over A few minutes
Medium Coarse sand Flat-bottom drippers A few minutes
Coarse Sea salt French press Several minutes
Extra coarse Cracked peppercorns Cold brew Many hours

Contact time drives the choice. Grinding finer exposes more surface area, so water dissolves flavour faster. In a percolation method such as pour-over, a finer grind also slows the flow, which lengthens the brew. A short brew needs a fine grind to extract enough, while a long immersion needs a coarse grind so extraction does not run into harsh, bitter compounds.

Coffee Water Temperature: The Best Range for Pour-Over and How to Hit It

Passed the gate First draft Review 5/4/5/4

For pour-over coffee, aim for water between roughly 90 and 96 °C. Lighter roasts usually want the hotter end of that window and medium roasts the cooler end. Water much cooler than this tends to give a sour, thin cup. Below, we explain why temperature matters, how to match it to the coffee in your dripper, and the practical ways to measure and hold it at home.

The short answer: what temperature should coffee water be?

The range most brewing guides settle on for filter coffee is 90–96 °C, which sits just below boiling point at sea level. It is a window rather than a single number because coffees differ in how readily they give up their flavour.

Boiling water is not automatically wrong. With very light, dense coffees, pouring close to the boil can work well. With more soluble coffees, though, water that hot often pulls out harsh, bitter and drying compounds alongside the good ones.

At the other end, water that has fallen well below 90 °C tends to under-extract, leaving a cup that tastes sour, thin and short on sweetness. Remember that water starts cooling the moment it leaves the kettle, and loses more heat when it meets a cold dripper, a paper filter and the grounds themselves.

Why temperature matters: extraction in plain terms

Brewing coffee is a matter of dissolving flavour compounds out of ground beans. Hotter water dissolves them faster and more completely; cooler water works more slowly and may never reach the later, sweeter compounds within a normal brew time.

The compounds do not all come out at the same rate. Bright acids dissolve early, sugars and heavier body follow, and the more bitter, astringent compounds arrive last. Temperature shifts how far along that sequence you get in the time the water spends with the coffee.

That is why temperature never acts alone. It works together with:

  • Grind size: finer grounds expose more surface area and extract faster.
  • Contact time: a longer brew extracts more, a shorter one less.
  • Ratio: the amount of water relative to coffee changes strength and how much gets dissolved. A common starting point for pour-over is around 1:15 to 1:17 coffee to water by weight.

A simple temperature routine to try this week

The quickest way to understand temperature is to taste it. This side-by-side test isolates temperature so you can hear what it does to a single coffee.

  1. Pick one coffee and grind once. Grind enough for both brews at the same setting so grind size cannot muddy the result.
  2. Fix your dose and ratio. Use the same weights for both cups, for example 15 g of coffee to 250 g of water.
