How to Make Cold Brew Coffee at Home: Beans, Grind, Ratio and Steep Time

Passed the gate Repaired once Review 5/4/5/4 Agent's grade: would publish

Agent's grade. Useful and specific: explains why cold brew tastes different, gives grind, ratio and steep guidance, and turns product facts into practical advice (the grinder holds 25 g, so a full basket takes several rounds). Product claims match their facts. Long at about 1,600 words, and a few general points repeat; a human editor would trim the last section.

To make cold brew coffee, steep coarsely ground coffee in cold water for many hours, then remove the grounds and dilute the concentrate to taste. Most home recipes call for a steep of roughly 12 to 24 hours in the fridge or at room temperature. Four variables decide the result: the beans, the grind size, the ratio of coffee to water and the steep time. Get those right and the rest is simply patience.

What cold brew is and why it tastes different

Cold brew is an immersion method. Instead of water passing through a bed of coffee, as in pour-over, the grounds sit fully submerged in the water for the whole brew. The difference from a hot immersion brew such as a French press is temperature: cold water extracts far more slowly, so a brew that takes minutes when hot takes hours when cold.

That slow, cool extraction changes the flavour. Hot water pulls out acids and volatile aromatics quickly, along with some of the harsher compounds that read as bitterness. Cold water extracts more selectively, so cold brew usually tastes rounder, sweeter and lower in perceived acidity, with a heavier, almost syrupy body.

It is worth separating cold brew from iced coffee. Iced coffee is coffee brewed hot and then chilled or poured over ice, which keeps the brightness and aromatics of a hot brew but can taste thin or sharp once diluted. Cold brew never meets hot water at all, which is why the two drinks taste so different even when both are served over ice.

You can make cold brew in almost any vessel, but a dedicated brewer makes the process cleaner. Our Cold Brew Jar is an immersion brewer made by Fernhill. It is a glass jar with a capacity of 1.5 litre and a fine stainless steel filter basket.

Choosing beans for cold brew

Because cold extraction mutes acidity and emphasises body and sweetness, the classic choice is a medium roast with chocolate, caramel and nut character. These coffees are forgiving: even if your ratio or steep time is a little off, the cup stays smooth and sweet. Lighter, fruit-forward roasts work too, but they give a brighter, more unusual cold brew, which some people love and others find surprising.

In our range, the classic-style pick is Colombia Huila, washed, a washed-process, medium-roast single origin. Its tasting notes are red apple, caramel and milk chocolate, in line with the caramel and chocolate character described above. It comes in 250 g and 1 kg bags, and the larger size makes sense if cold brew becomes a regular habit.

For something more adventurous, Ethiopia Guji, natural process is a light roast with notes of blueberry, dark chocolate and jasmine.

A pre-ground filter grind is too fine for a steep that lasts many hours, and it will push your concentrate towards bitterness. Grind coarse, like for French press

Grind size matters more in cold brew than in almost any other method, simply because the coffee sits in water for so long. Small differences in particle size are magnified over a long steep.

  • Too fine: the coffee over-extracts, tasting bitter, woody or harsh, and fine particles cloud the concentrate and clog the filter, giving a muddy result.
  • Too coarse: the water cannot extract enough from each particle, and the concentrate tastes weak, sour or hollow even after a long steep.
  • Just right: a coarse, even grind similar to what you would use for a French press, with particles roughly the size of coarse sea salt.

A burr grinder gives the most even particle size, which is what keeps cold brew clean. Our Hand Grinder uses conical steel burrs and has external click adjustment with 30 settings. Its settings run from espresso to French press, so the coarse end of the dial reaches the French press grind that cold brew calls for.

Be aware of one practical point. The Hand Grinder holds 25 g of beans at a time, so a full basket of up to 150 g for the Cold Brew Jar takes several rounds of grinding. Set your coarse setting once, then grind in batches into the same container so every load matches. More grinders, including electric models, are in our grinders collection.

How much coffee and how long to steep

Cold brew is usually made as a concentrate, then diluted before drinking, though you can also brew it ready to drink. Recipes vary widely, but as a general guide, measured by weight:

  • Concentrate: somewhere between about 1:4 and 1:10 coffee to water, depending on how strong you want it before diluting.
  • Ready to drink: around 1:12 to 1:15 coffee to water.

Weighing both the coffee and the water with a scale, rather than using scoops and jug markings, is what lets you repeat a good batch and adjust a disappointing one with precision. Note the weight of water you add each time so you can work out your ratio. Change one variable at a time, either the dose or the steep time, so you know which change made the difference.

The Cold Brew Jar is built around a concentrate batch. Its filter basket holds up to 150 g of coarse ground coffee, and the jar makes 1.5 litres of concentrate. The recommended steep time is 12 to 18 hours in the fridge. Begin in the middle of that window, taste, and adjust on the next batch: steep longer if the concentrate tastes thin or sour, and shorter if it tastes bitter or flat. More coffee for the same amount of water gives a stronger concentrate, while a lighter dose gives a softer one you can dilute less.

Step by step with the Cold Brew Jar

  1. Weigh and grind. Weigh your beans, up to the basket's limit of 150 g, and grind them coarse.
  2. Fill the basket. Tip the grounds into the fine stainless steel filter basket and level them gently.
  3. Add water slowly. Pour cold, filtered water slowly over the grounds so every particle is wetted; dry pockets extract unevenly. Give the grounds a gentle stir if any stay dry on top.
  4. Seal it. Close the airtight lid.
  5. Refrigerate. The jar fits in a fridge door.
  6. Steep. Leave it for 12 to 18 hours in the fridge, as recommended.
  7. Lift out the basket. When the time is up, remove the filter basket and let it drain. Taking the grounds out of the water is what stops extraction, so do not leave the basket in once the steep is done.

What is left in the jar is your concentrate, ready to dilute and serve.

Diluting, serving and storing your concentrate

A good starting point is to dilute concentrate about 1:1 with cold water or milk, poured over ice. From there, adjust to taste: add more water for a lighter, more refreshing glass, or less for something closer to an iced espresso drink. Milk and oat milk both pair well with the chocolatey sweetness of a medium-roast cold brew.

A few ideas once you have the basics:

  • Cold brew tonic: pour concentrate over tonic water and ice for a bitter-sweet, sparkling drink that suits fruity, light-roast coffees especially well.
  • Hot cold brew: dilute the concentrate with just-boiled water for a smooth, low-acidity hot cup.
  • Sweetened: stir in simple syrup rather than granulated sugar, which dissolves poorly in cold liquid.

For storage, keep the concentrate sealed and cold. The Cold Brew Jar has an airtight lid. Undiluted concentrate is best drunk fresh, within several days of brewing, as its flavour fades the longer it is kept. Dilute only what you are about to drink, as diluted cold brew loses its character faster.

No dedicated jar? Use a French press

If you already own a French press, or want one brewer that handles both hot and cold coffee, you can make cold brew in it. Our French Press is an immersion brewer with a capacity of 1 litre, a smaller batch than our 1.5 litre Cold Brew Jar.

  1. Add coarse grounds to the beaker and pour in cold, filtered water, stirring gently so everything is wet.
  2. Rest the lid on top without pressing, cover the spout, and steep it in the fridge.
  3. When the steep is done, press the plunger down slowly.
  4. Decant the coffee into a separate sealed bottle straight away, because coffee left sitting on the grounds keeps extracting and turns bitter.

The French Press has a four-layer mesh filter. Pour gently and leave the last of the liquid behind to keep any stray fines out of your bottle. Afterwards, all parts of the French Press come apart for cleaning. It and the Cold Brew Jar are both immersion brewers, and you can find them in our immersion brewers collection.

French Press 1 litre

Whichever brewer you use, the method is the same: good beans, an even coarse grind, a weighed ratio and a patient steep. Nail those four, and a fridge full of smooth homemade cold brew is only ever one evening's prep away.
