To use a French press, weigh about 1 g of coffee for every 15–16 g of water, grind it coarse, pour water just off the boil, steep for about 4 minutes, skim the crust, then plunge slowly and pour out every drop at once. Get those variables right and you get the heavy, rounded body the method is known for, without a layer of sludge at the bottom of the mug. The rest of this guide covers each step and the reasoning behind it, plus how to keep your press clean.

Why the French press tastes the way it does

A French press is an immersion brewer. Instead of water flowing through a bed of coffee, as it does in pour-over, all of the grounds sit in all of the water for the whole brew. Extraction is even and forgiving, and it is controlled mostly by grind size and time.

The other half of the character comes from the filter. A paper filter traps coffee oils and very fine particles, while a metal mesh lets a good share of them through. Those oils and fines are what give French press coffee its weight and texture. Handled badly, the same fines turn the cup muddy and gritty, which is why grind and technique matter so much.

What you need: ratio, dose and batch size

A reliable starting ratio is 1:15 to 1:16 by weight. That works out at roughly 62–67 g of coffee per litre of water. Use the stronger end if you like a dense, syrupy cup and the weaker end for something lighter and easier to drink.

Weigh both the coffee and the water rather than using scoops. Bean size, density and roast level all change how much coffee a scoop holds, so volume measures make it hard to repeat a good brew. A kitchen scale makes the whole process consistent.

Our French Press has a capacity of 1 litre and makes about 4 mugs. You do not have to fill it every time; scale the dose to the water instead:

  • 500 ml of water: about 31–33 g of coffee.
  • 750 ml of water: about 47–50 g of coffee.
  • 1 litre of water: about 62–67 g of coffee.

Grind coarse and grind fresh

Grind size is the single biggest lever in French press brewing. A coarse grind, roughly like coarse sea salt, slows extraction so the coffee does not turn bitter over a long steep. Just as important, coarse particles are too big to pass through the mesh, so fewer fines end up suspended in your cup.

Evenness matters as much as size. A blade grinder smashes beans into a mix of boulders and dust, and that dust slips straight through the filter. A burr grinder cuts beans to a far more uniform size, which gives a cleaner cup and more predictable extraction. Grind just before you brew, too: ground coffee loses aroma quickly, and the difference is obvious in a method that showcases body and flavour as directly as this one.

Step by step: brewing your French press

With your coffee weighed and ground, the brew itself is simple. The details below are what separate a clean cup from a muddy one.

  • Preheat the beaker. Fill it with hot water, swirl, and discard. A warm beaker keeps the brew temperature stable instead of pulling heat out of the water.
  • Add the grounds. Tip them in and give the beaker a gentle shake to level the bed.
  • Pour water just off the boil. Aim for roughly 93–96 °C. Without a thermometer, letting a boiled kettle rest for about 30 seconds gets you close. Pour steadily so all the grounds are soaked.
  • Stir gently. A brief stir makes sure no dry clumps remain. Set the lid on with the plunger pulled all the way up to hold in the heat.
  • Steep. Leave it for about 4 minutes. A crust of grounds and foam will form on the surface.
  • Break the crust and skim. Give the crust a gentle stir so most of it sinks, then spoon off the remaining foam and floating grounds. This removes much of the fine material that would otherwise end up in your cup.
  • Let it settle (optional). For an even cleaner result, wait a few more minutes so the fines drift to the bottom. Extraction slows a lot once the grounds have sunk, so the cup gets clearer rather than harsher.
  • Plunge slowly. Press the plunger down gently and stop just above the settled grounds. On our French Press, the four-layer mesh filter does this separating. Forcing any plunger down fast stirs up the bed and pushes fines through the mesh.
  • Pour it all out at once. Decant every drop into mugs or a carafe straight away. In an immersion brewer, coffee left sitting on the grounds keeps extracting and turns bitter. Leave the last spoonful in the beaker, where the heaviest sediment collects.

Cleaning and care

A French press stays good only if it stays clean. Tip spent grounds into the compost or the bin rather than the sink, where they build up in pipes. Rinse the mesh filter under running water after every brew so grounds do not dry into it.

To make cold brew coffee, steep coarsely ground coffee in cold water for many hours, then remove the grounds and dilute the concentrate to taste. Four variables decide the result: the beans, the grind size, the ratio of coffee to water and the steep time. Get those right and the rest is simply patience.

What cold brew is and why it tastes different

Cold brew is an immersion method. Instead of water passing through a bed of coffee, as in pour-over, the grounds sit fully submerged in the water for the whole brew. The difference from a hot immersion brew such as a French press is temperature: cold water extracts far more slowly, so a brew that takes minutes when hot takes hours when cold.

That slow, cool extraction changes the flavour. Hot water pulls out acids and volatile aromatics quickly, along with some of the harsher compounds that read as bitterness. Cold water extracts more selectively, so cold brew usually tastes rounder, sweeter and lower in perceived acidity, with a heavier, almost syrupy body.

It is worth separating cold brew from iced coffee. Iced coffee is coffee brewed hot and then chilled or poured over ice, which keeps the brightness and aromatics of a hot brew but can taste thin or sharp once diluted. Cold brew never meets hot water at all, which is why the two drinks taste so different even when both are served over ice.

Grind coarse, like for French press

Grind size matters more in cold brew than in almost any other method, simply because the coffee sits in water for so long. Small differences in particle size are magnified over a long steep.

  • Too fine: the coffee over-extracts, tasting bitter, woody or harsh, and fine particles cloud the concentrate and clog the filter, giving a muddy result.
  • Too coarse: the water cannot extract enough from each particle, and the concentrate tastes weak, sour or hollow even after a long steep.
  • Just right: a coarse, even grind similar to what you would use for a French press, with particles roughly the size of coarse sea salt.

How much coffee and how long to steep

Cold brew is usually made as a concentrate, then diluted before drinking, though you can also brew it ready to drink. Recipes vary widely, but as a general guide, measured by weight:

  • Concentrate: somewhere between about 1:4 and 1:10 coffee to water, depending on how strong you want it before diluting.
  • Ready to drink: around 1:12 to 1:15 coffee to water.

Weighing both the coffee and the water with a scale, rather than using scoops and jug markings, is what lets you repeat a good batch and adjust a disappointing one with precision. Diluting, serving and storing your concentrate

A good starting point is to dilute concentrate about 1:1 with cold water or milk, poured over ice. From there, adjust to taste: add more water for a lighter, more refreshing glass, or less for something closer to an iced espresso drink. Milk and oat milk both pair well with the chocolatey sweetness of a medium-roast cold brew.

A few ideas once you have the basics:

  • Cold brew tonic: pour concentrate over tonic water and ice for a bitter-sweet, sparkling drink that suits fruity, light-roast coffees especially well.
  • Hot cold brew: dilute the concentrate with just-boiled water for a smooth, low-acidity hot cup.
  • Sweetened: stir in simple syrup rather than granulated sugar, which dissolves poorly in cold liquid.

For storage, keep the concentrate sealed and cold. The Cold Brew Jar has an airtight lid. Undiluted concentrate is best drunk fresh, within several days of brewing, as its flavour fades the longer it is kept. Dilute only what you are about to drink, as diluted cold brew loses its character faster.

The right grind size depends on contact time: the longer coffee and water stay together, the coarser the grind should be. Turkish coffee needs a powder as fine as flour. Espresso is fine. Contact time drives everything in this chart. Grinding finer exposes more surface area, so water pulls flavour out faster. A brew that lasts only seconds, like espresso, needs that speed. For pour-over coffee, aim for water between roughly 90 and 96 °C. Light roasts usually want the top of that window. Medium roasts often taste sweeter a few degrees lower. This guide explains why temperature matters, how to match it to the coffee you are brewing, and how to measure and hold it reliably at home.

The short answer: what temperature should coffee water be?

The range most often recommended for filter coffee is about 90–96 °C. Water in that band is hot enough to dissolve sweetness and body as well as acidity, without pulling out the harshest compounds too quickly.

Water straight off the boil is not automatically wrong. It loses heat on its way from the spout to the coffee bed, and a dense, light-roasted coffee can handle it well. The problem is that boiling water leaves no margin. With a fine grind or a long brew, it can push some coffees into bitterness and a drying finish.

At the other end, water well below 90 °C slows extraction noticeably. The result is typically a cup that tastes sour, thin and hollow, with a short finish, even when the beans themselves are excellent.

Why temperature matters: extraction in plain terms

Brewing coffee is a controlled dissolving process. Water pulls soluble compounds out of the ground coffee. Hotter water dissolves them faster and more completely.

The order in which flavours arrive matters too:

  • Acids come first. They dissolve readily, even at lower temperatures.
  • Sugars and heavier compounds follow. These give sweetness, body and length.
  • Bitter, astringent compounds come last. They build up when extraction runs too far.

Temperature never acts alone. It works together with grind size and contact time. A finer grind exposes more surface area, and a longer brew gives the water more time to work. Hotter water speeds up both effects. So if you raise the temperature, you may need to grind slightly coarser to land in the same place. If you lower it, a slightly finer grind can compensate.

Matching temperature to roast level

Roast level is the best single guide to where to start within the window.

Light roasts are denser and less soluble, because they spend less time in the roaster. They generally need hotter water, around 93–96 °C, to extract fully and avoid tasting grassy or sour.

Medium roasts are more developed and dissolve more easily. They often taste sweeter and rounder a few degrees lower, around 90–93 °C, where extra heat would otherwise start to emphasise roast bitterness.

A szálas gyógynövényeket általában száraz, fénytől védett helyen, jól záródó edényben érdemes tartani. Kerüld a tűzhely közelét és a párás helyeket, mert a nedvesség és a meleg ronthatja a tea minőségét. Ha nincs otthon megfelelő tárolódobozod, nézz szét teaeszközeink között.

Ennyi az egész: kimérsz egy teáskanálnyit, felöntöd a megfelelő hőfokú vízzel, kivárod az áztatási időt, és leszűröd. Jó teázást!

Reviewer's remarks

  • “Rozsdamentes acél hálóból készült, amelynek lyukbősége 0,3 mm, így a finomabb teadarabok is a szűrőben maradnak [F13, F14].” — A tények csak a lyukbőséget adják meg. Azt, hogy a finom darabokat is visszatartja, egyik tény sem állítja, ez hozzáadott termékelőny.
  • “A 7–9 cm átmérőjű bögrékbe illik, két füle pedig a bögre peremén nyugszik [F15, F16].” — F15 csak azt mondja, hogy két füle van. Hogy a fülek a bögre peremén nyugszanak, a tényekből nem következik.
  • “Mivel szálas, vágott formában kapható, teáskanállal könnyen és egyenletesen ki tudod mérni [F1, F9].” — A „könnyen és egyenletesen” kimérhetőséget a tények nem támasztják alá, ez túlzó következtetés.
  • “Az alapanyag eredete és feldolgozása sokat számít: egy gondosan gyűjtött és szárított gyógynövény a csészében is jobban megmutatja a karakterét.” — Homályos dicséret és töltelék, konkrét információt nem ad.
  • “Áztatás közben érdemes lefedni a csészét, a végén pedig csak le kell szűrnöd, és már kortyolhatod is.” — A kakukkfű teánál a lefedés nem szerepel a tényekben (fedő alatti áztatást csak a kamillánál ír F39). Ha általános tanács, sincs így jelölve.
